We highlight a key factor contributing to this limitation: models frequently arrive at correct final answers despite misperceiving visual information during intermediate steps. We define this phenomenon as Correct-but-Visually-Misgrounded Reasoning (CVMR). Because standard RLVR rewards only final-answer correctness, it fails to differentiate between properly grounded and misgrounded reasoning, reinforcing both equally. Through token-level analysis, we reveal that CVMR tokens exhibit distinct attention patterns that differ systematically from well-grounded ones. Based on this observation, we propose GRIT, a training framework that incorporates visual grounding into RLVR to mitigate the impact of CVMR. Furthermore, to address the lack of text-rich reasoning benchmarks that allow for systematic evaluation across text length and reasoning depth, we introduce GRIT-Bench. These methods have demonstrated significant performance improvements across various visual reasoning tasks. Beyond general visual perception, text-rich reasoning, which involves reading and reasoning over text in images such as financial reports, medical documents, and charts, has emerged as a key capability of VLMs聽 [ 27 , 21 , 19 , 37 ] . These tasks require multi-step reasoning far exceeding simple text recognition, integrating textual content with visual context聽 [ 13 , 19 , 33 , 31 ] . However, RLVR-driven improvements do not consistently extend to this domain. Despite numerous recent efforts to enhance the visual reasoning capabilities of VLMs through RL聽 [ 14 , 22 , 32 , 4 , 16 , 35 , 24 ] , there has been relatively limited systematic analysis of text-rich reasoning. For example, Huang et聽al. [13] recently reported that RL-trained models perform even worse than baselines on text-rich reasoning tasks, and we observe similar degradation on text-rich reasoning benchmarks, including OCR-Reasoning, in our experiments. To investigate the underlying causes of this degradation, we closely examine VLM responses and identify a critical phenomenon we term Correct-but-Visually-Misgrounded Reasoning (CVMR), where models frequently arrive at correct answers despite misperceiving visual information (additional examples in Supplementary Sec.聽 E ). Unlike standard hallucinations聽 [ 17 ] , this failure mode bypasses answer-only verification because the final output remains correct. Text-rich reasoning tasks are structurally vulnerable to CVMR due to their dense visual elements. Our analysis shows that CVMR occurs at a disproportionately high rate in these tasks (Section聽 3 ), suggesting that standard RLVR, which primarily relies on answer correctness, creates a systematic risk of reinforcing misgrounded reasoning. To address this, we propose GRIT ( Gr adient Masking for V i sually Misgrounded T okens). We observe that CVMR tokens exhibit weak or diffuse attention over relevant image regions. To capture such attention patterns, GRIT selects a subset of attention heads that sensitively reflect the image regions attended by each generated token, termed Visual Tracker Heads . During training, GRIT analyzes the attention patterns from these heads to identify visually misgrounded tokens and masks their gradients to prevent CVMR from being reinforced. To incorporate the faithfulness of visual grounding into the training signal, we introduce an attention reward based on their attention mass and entropy. Consequently, as shown in Figure聽 1 , while existing RLVR methods largely degrade or show marginal improvement on text-rich reasoning benchmarks, GRIT achieves substantial gains. Furthermore, systematically evaluating the extent to which models rely on correct visual information requires more than answer-level supervision; it requires verifying whether each reasoning step is faithfully grounded in the visual input. Existing text-rich reasoning benchmarks provide valuable testbeds, but they rarely offer the fine-grained reference information needed to assess visual grounding throughout the reasoning process. To enable such analysis, we introduce GRIT-Bench , a benchmark constructed by rendering well-established text-based QA datasets into images. This design yields two advantages. First, it provides a controlled testbed for explicitly measuring the grounding fidelity of VLMs in text-rich reasoning. Second, because each visual input is paired with its exact source text, an LLM judge can evaluate the VLM鈥檚 reasoning process directly against this text, providing a more reliable basis than image-only evaluation for assessing whether visual information was correctly perceived. Motivated by this, recent work has extended RLVR to VLMs. Vision-R1a聽 [ 14 ] applies GRPO-based training with a synthetically constructed multimodal CoT dataset and introduces progressive thinking suppression to reduce overthinking. Vision-R1b聽 [ 38 ] proposes vision-guided reinforcement learning with progressive rule refinement for human-free alignment. MM-Eureka聽 [ 22 ] demonstrates that minimalist rule-based rewards combined with a curated multimodal mathematics dataset are sufficient for effective multimodal reasoning. VL-Rethinker聽 [ 32 ] proposes forced rethinking, which appends rethinking triggers to rollouts to encourage explicit self-reflection during RL training. VLAA-Thinker聽 [ 4 ] investigates the SFT-then-RL paradigm and finds that direct RL with a mixed reward module integrating perception and cognition signals outperforms the conventional pipeline. Vision-SR1聽 [ 16 ] proposes a self-rewarding framework that decomposes reasoning into visual perception and language reasoning, using the model itself to evaluate its own visual perception quality. While these methods have demonstrated improvements on general visual reasoning benchmarks, their effectiveness on text-rich reasoning remains limited, as we observe in our experiments. GRIT addresses this gap by incorporating visual grounding into the RLVR training process. 2.2 Text-Rich Visual Reasoning Existing benchmarks for text-rich image understanding, such as TextVQA聽 [ 27 ] , DocVQA聽 [ 21 ] , and ChartQA聽 [ 19 ] , have contributed to evaluating text recognition and comprehension in various visual contexts. However, these benchmarks are largely centered on textual content extraction, where answers can often be directly retrieved from the OCR results of the image. OCR-Reasoning聽 [ 13 ] addresses this gap by constructing a benchmark where answers are minimally present in the OCR output, requiring the model to perform reasoning rather than extraction. It systematically defines six core reasoning sub-abilities for text-rich scenarios, including spatial, numerical, mathematical, logical, enumerative, and multidisciplinary reasoning, providing a structured evaluation framework that was previously absent. These characteristics make OCR-Reasoning a useful testbed for examining whether RL-based multimodal reasoning methods improve text-rich reasoning beyond mere text extraction. ChartQA-PRO聽 [ 20 ] and CharXiv聽 [ 33 ] also evaluate chart and figure understanding, which partially involves text-rich reasoning. GRIT-Bench complements these benchmarks by enabling fine-grained analysis across text length and reasoning depth while additionally measuring reasoning fidelity. 3 CVMR Analysis Figure 3 : CVMR rate among correct responses by task type. Text-rich reasoning exhibits a substantially higher CVMR rate compared to other task types. As introduced in Section聽 1 , CVMR refers to the phenomenon where a model arrives at the correct final answer despite misperceiving visual information in the image. In this section, we quantitatively analyze which task types are most susceptible to CVMR and investigate the distinct attention patterns exhibited by tokens associated with CVMR. To systematically analyze the occurrence of CVMR, we collect 600 samples across four groups defined by two axes: text density and task type, with 150 correct samples per group. Since CVMR by definition occurs exclusively in correct responses, we restrict our samples accordingly. Images containing 200 or more characters of text are classified as text-rich, and those with fewer as text-sparse. Tasks requiring at least two reasoning steps to solve are classified as reasoning, while those answerable by simply locating a value in the image are classified as recognition. Samples are drawn from ChartQA聽 [ 19 ] , ChartQAPro聽 [ 20 ] , DocVQA聽 [ 21 ] , ScienceQA聽 [ 18 ] , A-OKVQA聽 [ 25 ] , and RealWorldQA聽 [ 34 ] , with responses generated by Qwen2.5-VL-7B聽 [ 3 ] . CVMR is determined for each sample by first using GPT-5聽 [ 28 ] to judge whether the model鈥檚 reasoning process contains misperceptions of visual information, followed by human double-verification. Figure聽 3 shows the CVMR rate among correct responses by task type. The CVMR rate in text-rich reasoning is 35.3%, substantially higher than that of text-sparse reasoning (14.0%), text-sparse recognition (10.7%), and text-rich recognition (4.7%). This result stems from the structural characteristics of text-rich reasoning. Text-rich reasoning tasks contain numerous visual elements that must be read and interpreted, and misperceptions of elements not directly tied to the final answer during multi-step reasoning can still yield correct responses. In contrast, text-rich recognition exhibits the lowest CVMR rate: the retrieved value itself constitutes the answer without multi-step reasoning, meaning any misreading directly leads to an incorrect answer. Because standard RLVR primarily relies on answer correctness as its reward signal, it cannot distinguish visually misgrounded tokens from properly grounded ones. Consequently, tasks with higher CVMR rates risk having misgrounded reasoning reinforced alongside correct ones. We further compare the attention characteristics of text tokens where visual misperceptions occur, which we term CVMR tokens, against normal tokens that correctly reflect visual information. Specifically, we utilize Visual Tracker Heads (Section聽 4.2 ) to analyze two metrics: visual attention mass and visual attention entropy. Visual attention mass represents the total attention a generated text token allocates to image tokens, while visual attention entropy captures whether the attention is focused on specific image regions or spread broadly. For example, a higher attention mass indicates that the model references the image more, while a lower attention entropy indicates more focused attention on specific image regions聽 [ 15 , 40 ] . As shown in Figure聽 4 , we find that CVMR tokens exhibit a distinct attention pattern compared to normal tokens, characterized by lower visual attention mass and higher attention entropy (see Supplementary Sec.聽 E for qualitative examples). This pattern is consistent across both metrics, suggesting that CVMR tokens do not sufficiently focus on relevant image regions. This observation shows that attention patterns can serve as a meaningful signal for distinguishing CVMR tokens, motivating the design of GRIT. 4 GRIT In Section聽 3 , we observed that CVMR tokens exhibit lower visual attention mass and higher attention entropy. Since RLVR assigns the same reward to all tokens within a correct response, it cannot distinguish visually grounded tokens from misgrounded ones, causing CVMR to be reinforced alongside correct reasoning. In this section, we introduce GRIT, a training framework that addresses this limitation. GRIT consists of three components: (1) Visual Tracker Heads, which select attention heads that reflect token-level variation in visual attention patterns, (2) gradient masking, which blocks gradients for misgrounded tokens based on the attention patterns of selected heads, and (3) attention reward, which incorporates the faithfulness of visual grounding into the reward signal. 4.1 Group Relative Policy Optimization We adopt GRPO聽 [ 26 ] as the base RL algorithm. Since GRIT masks gradients at the token level (Section聽 4.3 ), it requires a way to assess whether each individual token is visually grounded. This in turn demands attention heads that sensitively reflect which image region each generated token references. If a head attends to the same image region regardless of which token is being generated, it cannot distinguish well-grounded tokens from misgrounded ones. We select heads satisfying this property and refer to them as Visual Tracker Heads . We propose a two-stage procedure for their identification. Mass Filtering. The selection is performed on 1,000 samples drawn from聽 [ 19 , 20 , 21 , 18 , 25 , 34 ] . Since only a small fraction of attention heads contribute meaningfully to text-image interaction, we first compute the mean visual attention mass across all response tokens for each head, and retain the top 50 heads that effectively attend to image tokens. Diversity Ranking. Among the retained heads, we identify those whose attention distribution over image tokens changes most across generated tokens. Such heads are informative because their token-level variation enables per-token grounding assessment. Further details on Visual Tracker Heads are provided in Supplementary Sec.聽 B . 4.3 Gradient Masking In standard RLVR, all tokens within a response share the same response-level advantage, so visually misgrounded tokens in a positively reinforced correct response receive the same policy credit as grounded tokens. To prevent this, GRIT selectively blocks gradients for tokens that are likely to be visually misgrounded, using the attention patterns of Visual Tracker Heads. Two-Stage Token Filtering. For each token in a correct response, we compute its visual attention mass and entropy on the Visual Tracker Heads. Within each response, tokens suspected of being misgrounded are identified through two successive filters: 1. Low Mass Filter : tokens in the bottom 50% of visual attention mass within the response are selected. 2. High Entropy Filter : among those, tokens in the top 50% of visual attention entropy are selected. The gradients of the resulting tokens are masked. This filtering is intentionally approximate and conservative: masking removes a token鈥檚 policy-loss contribution at the current update rather than applying a gradient in the opposite direction. This filtering uses attention statistics as a proxy for token-level grounding. In particular, no benchmark allows fine-grained analysis of model performance by text length or reasoning depth. This is because constructing problems that are text-rich and require multi-step reasoning is inherently difficult, and ground truth verification is challenging聽 [ 13 ] . GRIT-Bench addresses this by rendering text-based QA datasets into images. The original source text is preserved prior to rendering. During evaluation, the judge (GPT-5 mini) receives this source text rather than the image to analyze the model鈥檚 reasoning process. This enables direct measurement of whether the model correctly read the information in the image. Specifically, well-established table and document QA datasets聽 [ 6 , 42 , 8 , 9 , 23 , 7 , 39 ] are rendered into images for use as VLM inputs. Text lengths range from 238 to 42,343 characters (mean 3,136), and samples with excessive text are split into multiple images to avoid resolution issues. For Reasoning Fidelity (RF) evaluation, key reasoning steps for each sample are extracted by GPT-5 from the source text. Human double-checking is performed to verify both that the answer is derivable from the rendered image and that the reasoning steps are correct. GRIT-Bench evaluates with two metrics: Accuracy and Reasoning Fidelity (RF). Accuracy measures final answer correctness. RF evaluates the visual grounding faithfulness of the reasoning process, defined as: RF = n faithful n total + n ungrounded \mathrm{RF}=\frac{n_{\mathrm{faithful}}}{n_{\mathrm{total}}+n_{\mathrm{ungrounded}}} (10) where n faithful n_{\mathrm{faithful}} is the number of key reasoning steps that the model attempted and that are consistent with the source text, n total n_{\mathrm{total}} is the total number of key reasoning steps, and n ungrounded n_{\mathrm{ungrounded}} is the number of claims not supported by the source text beyond the key steps. A higher RF indicates that the reasoning process more accurately reflects the information in the image and less frequently asserts content absent from the source. GRIT-Bench classifies difficulty along two axes: text length and reasoning depth. Text length is divided into Short ( < < 1K), Medium (1鈥?K), and Long (2K+), and reasoning depth into Simple (2 steps), Moderate (3), and Complex (4+). The two axes form 9 difficulty combinations over 2,855 samples (Table聽 1 ). Details on dataset composition are provided in Supplementary Sec.聽 D . Appendix C Efficient Attention Weight Extraction GRIT requires attention weights only during training. Token-level masking itself is a negligible Boolean operation on the policy loss; the measured overhead comes from materializing attention weights, which prevents the selected layers from using the default FlashAttention-style implementation. A naive implementation extracts attention weights from every layer. However, the five Visual Tracker Heads used by GRIT are distributed across only five of the 28 layers in Qwen2.5-VL-7B. We therefore extract attention weights only from these five layers and retain the default efficient attention implementation in all remaining layers. Appendix F Discussion and Future Work Approximate token filtering. GRIT uses visual attention mass and entropy as a lightweight proxy for token-level grounding. The resulting mask is conservative: selected tokens are excluded from the current policy update rather than optimized in the opposite direction. Tables聽 5 and聽 6 show that the gains depend on attention-based token selection and remain stable across different masking thresholds and numbers of Visual Tracker Heads. Nevertheless, attention statistics do not perfectly determine whether an individual token is visually grounded. Future work could replace the fixed percentile rule with a calibrated or learned grounding-confidence estimator. Training efficiency. The additional training cost arises from materializing attention weights, rather than from applying the token mask itself. By extracting attention only from the five layers containing Visual Tracker Heads, GRIT reduces the relative wall-clock cost from 2.52 脳 2.52\times for all-layer extraction to 1.29 脳 1.29\times , while preserving the original model architecture and inference cost. More selective attention kernels could further reduce this overhead. Scope of GRIT-Bench. GRIT-Bench uses rendered inputs to preserve exact source鈥搃mage alignment, enabling source-aligned RF evaluation and controlled analysis by text length and reasoning depth. This controlled setting does not capture the full variability of real-world layouts, typography, and capture noise. We therefore use GRIT-Bench together with independently constructed text-rich benchmarks, including OCR-Reasoning, ChartQA-PRO, and CharXiv. Future work could extend RF-style evaluation to real documents with verified source text and step-level references.
